## Three findings

1. **Compressibility IS the transfer mechanism.** Zero-referee retention R(0) — learning B alone,
   no rehearsal — rises smoothly from the random floor 0.088 to 0.998 as rank falls. When new and old
   knowledge share a rule, fitting B *reconstructs* A. The catastrophic-forgetting regime is exactly
   the incompressible corner; structure converts most of it to free transfer.

2. **The referee gives a real but modest above-chord lift (max ~+0.05 at mid-rank, budget 0.5).** A
   small anchor set protects somewhat more old decisions than its literal membership — genuine
   compression via shared weights — but the increment is small next to the transfer term. Rehearsal
   is the minor mechanism here; transfer is the major one.

3. **Low-margin selection reliably beats stratified random** on compressible tasks (e.g. k=8: +0.050
   vs +0.018), unlike the random-label task where it was useless. So the fragility heuristic has
   something to exploit once facts share structure — partial answer to your "don't expect low-margin
   to win" caution: it does win here, modestly.

The generic MLP (not a matched factorized student) shows the transfer, so this is your stronger case:
**learner-accessible compressibility** — sequential consolidation *discovers* the shared rule, it
isn't just handed the right representation.

Honest caveats: class balance is still skewed at low k (25 at k=1), so k=1–2 transfer is partly
majority-class; the trend and the k=4–16 points (balance 3.7–7.8, well above chance) are solid. Lift
is modest; a coverage-based selector (your suggestion) might raise it and I haven't built it yet.

## The reframing, and why your rule+exceptions experiment is the right next step

This flips the problem statement. For compressible knowledge the dominant retention mechanism is
**transfer, not rehearsal** — and the referee's marginal value should concentrate exactly on the
parts that DON'T obey the shared rule. That's your decomposed storage law. So I want to build it:

- Low-rank table (fixed k, say k=4 where transfer≈0.85), then replace a controlled fraction ρ of pair
  labels with fixed random **exceptions**. Sweep ρ ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5}.
- Prediction: report retention split into **rule-following pairs** vs **exception pairs**. Rule pairs
  retained via transfer at near-zero referee; exception pairs retained ~linearly in their *direct*
  referee support (the random law). The referee's above-chord lift should localize onto exceptions.
- **Growth trigger, made measurable:** after the shared rule saturates, the residual un-retained
  knowledge should look like unsupported exceptions — retention cost per exception ≈ the random slope.
  That's the signal that says "the remaining bits are incompressible → either pay linear memory or add
  capacity," rather than "grow whenever forgetting appears."
- I'll also finally add the **interference-oracle selector** (margin-drop after a provisional B step,
  as an explicitly illegal diagnostic) + a **coverage selector** over the frozen descriptors, since
  the exceptions regime is where selection should matter most.

Two questions:

1. For the exceptions experiment, should the exception labels be **per-pair random** (pure atomic
   noise, maximally incompressible) or **structured-but-off-rule** (e.g. a second low-rank rule of
   different k)? The former cleanly gives the linear law; the latter tests whether the system can
   discover a *second* rule vs treat it as noise — closer to real knowledge with sub-regularities. I
   lean pure-random first for the clean decomposition, then a second-rule variant.
2. Do you agree the headline claim so far should be: **"forgetting severity is a function of
   knowledge compressibility; on shared-structure knowledge, transfer carries retention and rehearsal
   is a small correction concentrated on incompressible exceptions"** — i.e. the interesting object is
   the *compressibility spectrum*, and growth is justified only for the incompressible residual after
   rule saturation?
