## Refinement 1 (load-bearing): a RANDOM-BASIS control, else we can't attribute the effect to interference

`nswrite` beating `naive` is not enough — projecting the gradient onto *any* smaller subspace also
shrinks/regularizes updates and can look like less forgetting. To prove the benefit comes from
protecting the **old-activation** directions specifically, add `nswrite_rand`: identical pipeline but
`U` is a **random orthonormal basis of the same per-module rank**. Claim requires
`nswrite ≫ nswrite_rand ≥ naive` on retention at **matched newest-learning**. Without this control the
result is unattributable (this is the R31/R23 discipline applied to the write mechanism).

## Refinement 2: we CAN measure interference for free at eval (old prompts allowed in eval, not in training)

Since the contract only forbids old prompts in the *training* loop, eval-time old prompts give us the
exact diagnostics that also become the phase-2 growth trigger — report them now:
- **gradient occupancy** `||G·UUᵀ|| / ||G||` per stream (the future saturation signal).
- **per-layer old-activation drift**: run old prompts through the checkpoint, compare trainable-layer
  outputs vs their commit-time values → tests failure mode #2 (basis staleness) directly.
- **answer-margin preservation**: old-fact logit(gold) − logit(runner-up), before/after each new
  stream → tests failure mode #4 (protecting hidden ≠ protecting the token) directly, not by faith.
These are cheap and turn your six failure modes into measured curves rather than speculation.

## Refinement 3: our setting is structurally FRIENDLIER than general CL — worth stating as the hypothesis

General null-space CL struggles partly because *all* layers are trainable, so old-prompt activations
themselves drift. In our stack only the **top-GROW layers are trainable and the entire lower stack is
frozen** → old-prompt activations *entering* the trainable block are **identical across streams by
construction**. So protecting the trainable Linears' response to those fixed inputs should preserve old
answers much more strongly than in the general case; residual drift is only the second-order
propagation *within* the 2-layer trainable block. This predicts `nswrite` should work *here* even if
generic null-space CL is fragile — and if it fails even here, that's a strong negative (interference in
this setting isn't linearly separable), which cleanly routes us to B (closed-form editing) rather than
more replay/growth. I want to state this as the falsifiable hypothesis for R36-I.

## Pushback on the pass bar (small): compare on the plasticity–stability FRONTIER, not single points

"`all_seen +0.20` and `newest ≥ 0.70`" can be gamed by any point on a tradeoff curve. I'd register:
`nswrite` must **dominate** `naive/ewc/nswrite_rand` — i.e., higher oldest/all-seen retention **at
equal-or-higher newest**. If it only trades newest for oldest, that's the classic stability–plasticity
move and I'll report it as *not* a solution (matching your intent, just stated as dominance not thresholds).

## Depth-vs-width is the real phase-2 prize (agree to defer, flag now)

Your failure mode #6 is the sharpest forward pointer: if saturation is *activation-dimension* saturation,
`grow_qwen` (identity depth) may not add the needed orthogonal capacity. So phase 2 shouldn't just be
"grow vs fixed" — it should be **depth-growth vs width/parallel-adapter growth at the saturation point**.
That would be the first *principled* statement about *what kind* of growth continual learning needs —
a genuinely new claim, and the cleanest possible resurrection of the growth thesis. Deferred, but it's
the target that makes R36-I worth doing.
