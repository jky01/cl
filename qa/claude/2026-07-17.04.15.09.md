## Your decomposition holds, plus two interactions

**Confirmed:** exceptions in the referee are perfectly retained (excIn=1.000); exceptions out of the
referee collapse to ~0.08 (≈ B-only exc floor 0.006–0.013, i.e. chance) — atomic exceptions are
unreconstructible and cost direct support, exactly the linear storage law. Rule pairs are largely
carried by reconstruction.

**Interaction 1 — exceptions poison rule discovery.** B-only reconstruction of the RULE pairs falls
monotonically with exception rate: 0.60 (rho=0) → 0.24 (rho=0.5). The atomic exceptions act as label
noise that degrades identification of the shared function itself. So exceptions are not merely a
separate additive storage cost; they *corrupt the compressible component's learnability*.

**Interaction 2 — rule and exceptions compete for the fixed shared substrate.** The exception-oracle
selector, given enough budget to hold all exceptions, retains them (excIn=1.0) but *damages the rule*:
at rho=0.5, budget=0.5 it drives ruleA down to 0.336 vs random's 0.703. Distilling ~1584 anti-rule
exception targets pulls the shared weights off the rule. There's a genuine frontier: concentrate on
exceptions → high exception retention, wrecked rule; spread → better rule, fewer exceptions kept. At
high exception density a fixed h=256 substrate cannot do both.

This is why the exception-oracle does NOT cleanly maximize allA at every point — at high rho it trades
the rule away for the exceptions. Low-margin ≈ random throughout (small differences), consistent with
your caution that fragility-proximity need not help.

## Two implications, and the growth experiment I'm building next

Implication for the north star ("find the function, don't memorize"): it's not enough to *want*
rule-discovery — you must **route** rule-following facts away from exceptions, because (1) mixing them
in poisons the rule and (2) they fight for capacity. That's a concrete argument for surprise-based
writing: learn the rule from predicted (low-surprise) facts, store only the high-surprise residual,
and keep the residual OUT of the rule's weights.

Implication for growth: interaction 2 is precisely a capacity-competition signal. So I'm building your
step 4 now — the growth intervention:

- Fixed-capacity (h) vs width-expanded learner, matched sequential data + referee budget + total
  optimization compute.
- Two residual types: (a) pure atomic exceptions (incompressible — growth should only move the linear
  memory bill into weights, NOT relieve the rule-vs-exception competition beyond raw capacity), and
  (b) a SECOND low-rank rule as the "exceptions" (compressible-but-off-first-rule — growth should let
  the model discover and internalize it, converting unsupported off-rule pairs into generalization).
- The decisive contrast: does added capacity raise retention of UNSUPPORTED off-rule pairs (real
  discovery) for (b) while doing nothing beyond linear storage for (a)? If yes, that's your growth
  trigger: grow when the residual is *structured* (a second rule) not when it's merely incompressible.

Two questions:

1. For the growth arm, should I grow width (more hidden units, same depth) or add a parallel
   module/adapter that is then folded in? You earlier wanted growth to remain ordinary weights with no
   inference-time routing. Width-expansion keeps that cleanly; a parallel module needs a fold step. I
   lean width-expansion for the clean capacity test, with the fold-a-module variant as a later arm.
2. To isolate interaction 1 (poisoning) from interaction 2 (competition), do you want a control where
   the rule is learned on CLEAN data first (no exceptions), frozen, and exceptions are added only in a
   separate capacity — so we measure competition without re-poisoning discovery? I think that cleanly
   separates the two effects and maps directly to "protect the discovered rule, store exceptions
   elsewhere."
