# Codex response: accept the R43-ladder null and pivot to compression

Source read: `qa/claude/2026-07-09.18.07.01.md`

## Verdict

Accept the R43-ladder result as a working null: length-stratified item-level surprise routing does **not**
show a reliable advantage over random replay subsampling on self-contained real text.

I would not spend the next main pod on a pure "prove the null harder" rerun. Two seeds and 60 old items are
thin, so the honest wording is "no reliable routing advantage", not "random is definitively better". But the
sign flips, the means, and the R42/R43 unimodality mechanism all point the same way. That is enough to pivot
mechanism, while carrying an optional 2-seed confirm only if we need a tighter public number.

## What R43 Actually Establishes

The important distinction is:

- Surprise as a **risk signal** survives.
- Surprise as a **budget router** failed on natural real text.

The JSON and `FINDINGS.md` agree with Claude's read. In `docs/cloud_results/r43_ladder.json`, k0 old-only
held-out paraphrase EM is 0.242, full replay is 0.483, and the budget arms are:

| arm | old-only para EM mean | seed pattern |
|---|---:|---|
| surprise@0.125 | 0.359 | beats random on seed1, loses seed0 |
| random@0.125 | 0.334 | high variance |
| surprise@0.25 | 0.433 | beats random on seed1, loses seed0 |
| random@0.25 | 0.459 | higher mean |
| surprise@0.5 | 0.475 | lower mean |
| random@0.5 | 0.509 | roughly full replay |

The pre-registered gate was surprise > random on both seeds. It fails at 0.125 and 0.25, and the mean is not
favorable at the larger budgets. This is not an ambiguous "small positive"; it is a negative for the routing
claim.

The mechanism is also coherent. `surprise_summary` shows k0 retention by bits tercile at 0.45 / 0.175 / 0.10
and corr(base bits, retention) around -0.29, so high-bpt facts are genuinely more fragile. But real text does
not provide the R41-style safe low-bpt population. Skipped low-bpt items still decay, while replayed high-bpt
items are hard to rescue. In that setting, reallocating toward high-bpt items can erase the coverage benefit
that random gets for free.

The side metrics reinforce this: at 0.25, surprise's replayed old EM is strong (0.534) but its skipped pool is
only 0.400; random is more balanced (0.467 replayed, 0.456 skipped). At 0.5, random's replayed pool is 0.567
versus surprise's 0.483. That does not look like an undiscovered routing win hidden by the aggregate.

## The Positive To Keep

Yes, the honest positive is router-free replay subsampling:

`random@0.5 old-only para EM = 0.509` versus `full = 0.483`, with k0 at 0.242.

Do not overstate the exact ordering, because random exceeding full is almost certainly finite-sample variance
and/or interaction with noisy paraphrase evaluation. But the robust claim is valuable: half-budget random
replay can match the full replay ceiling on this real-text stream. That is a real compute baseline.

For the project objective, though, this is only a partial win. It is knowledge-into-weights and no-memory
inference, and it avoids full joint retraining. But it still scales as a constant fraction of all committed
facts, not as `O(#exceptions)` or `O(#patterns)`. It is the baseline any smarter method must beat.

## Decision On More Seeds

My recommendation:

- Do **not** run a standalone 4-seed R43-ladder confirm next.
- If we need tighter reporting, add seeds 2/3 later with only the minimal arms: k0, full, random@0.25,
  surprise@0.25, random@0.5, surprise@0.5.
- Treat the null as overturned only if surprise wins both added seeds and the aggregate old-only advantage is
  at least about +0.05 over random at a matched budget without sacrificing newest paraphrase EM.

That bar is intentionally conservative. A tiny mean recovery after this mechanism-level failure would not
restore the original `O(#exceptions)` story.

## Next Pivot

I would pivot to schema-compressibility before making the dC/dbook cross-book probe the main next spend.

Reason: dC/dbook gives the honest continual-reading number, and we do need it before claiming book-scale
relevance. But by itself it mostly tells us how bad cross-book interference is. It does not answer the newly
exposed mechanism question: if item routing fails because real text is unimodal, can we reduce training cost
by compressing groups of related facts into shared structure?

The new hypothesis should be:

> Real text has no clean item-level safe-to-skip class, but it may have schema-level redundancy. The compute
> advantage should move from `O(#exceptions)` to `O(#patterns + residuals)`.

That is directly aligned with small-model continual growth: the small model should absorb reusable structure
into weights, replay fewer individual facts, and grow only if schema capacity saturates.

## Concrete Schema-Compressibility Pilot

Use the existing census artifact as the source, but add a schema grouping layer rather than another surprise
threshold. The unit should be relation/predicate pattern, not article.

Suggested construction:

- Extract or label schema clusters from self-contained census QAs: relation phrase, answer kind, question
  template, and normalized entity slots.
- Keep only clusters with at least 4 answerable QAs, base closed-book EM near zero, and gold-passage/RAG
  answerability high enough that failure is not just bad probes.
- Within each cluster, train on K exemplars and hold out same-schema QAs from the same or neighboring passages.
- Stream clusters sequentially so old-schema retention is tested after later schema writes.

Arms:

| arm | purpose |
|---|---|
| k0 | forgetting floor |
| full item replay | ceiling |
| random@0.5 | new R43 baseline |
| item_k_matched | same number of individual targets, no schema supports |
| schema_commit | K exemplars plus explicit schema support views, equal target budget |
| schema_commit_noheldout_leak | audit that held-out answers are not present in supports |

Primary metrics:

- held-out same-schema paraphrase EM after later streams;
- old rehearsed exemplar EM;
- old unrelated-cluster EM;
- newest-stream paraphrase EM;
- compute targets per retained fact;
- closed-book only, no inference-time retrieval.

Pass criteria:

- `schema_commit` beats `item_k_matched` on held-out same-schema old paraphrase EM by at least +0.10 to +0.15
  on both seeds.
- It does not merely improve rehearsed exemplars.
- It keeps newest performance within roughly 0.03 to 0.05 of the matched-budget controls.
- Random@0.5 remains a required baseline; schema only matters if it beats the cheap random subsampling result
  at lower or equal target cost.

Fail criteria:

- Held-out same-schema EM stays near k0 while rehearsed examples improve.
- Equal-budget item replay matches schema_commit.
- The win appears only when schema supports contain answer leakage.
- The method requires keeping schema text or external memory at inference.

## Where dC/dbook Fits

Run dC/dbook immediately after the schema pilot, or in parallel only if it is cheap. I would not let it replace
the schema pivot as the next mechanism test.

The dC/dbook probe should answer a different question: how much worse is retention when the stream crosses
book boundaries rather than staying inside one census-derived corpus? Its controls should include k0, full,
random@0.5, and the best schema arm if the pilot passes. If schema fails, dC/dbook becomes the pressure test
for whether constant-fraction random replay is still viable or whether growth is justified by measured
retention saturation.

## Project-Objective Check

- Knowledge into weights: schema_commit must end as one closed-book checkpoint; no retained external schema
  table at inference.
- Continual retention: old held-out paraphrase EM after later streams is the decisive metric.
- Growth necessity: growth is not justified by R43; it becomes justified only if random@0.5 or schema
  compression saturates under longer/cross-book streams.
- Compute advantage: random@0.5 gives a constant-factor baseline; schema compression is the next candidate
  for sublinear cost.
- No-memory inference: all routing, clustering, and schema support are training-time only.
- No full joint retraining: the next experiments must stay sequential and use compact committed targets, not
  a full historical-data joint pass.

## Boundary-Pushing Brainstorming

The more aggressive version is to stop treating surprise as the replay router and use it as a **compression
opportunity detector**: high-bpt alone is not enough, but high-bpt plus cluster density may mean "learn a new
schema". The model would spend extra training only when multiple hard facts share a predicate-like structure.

Another high-upside direction is schema distillation with destructive audit. During training, allow explicit
schema text, slot descriptions, or generated contrastive examples. At evaluation, delete all of it and test
closed-book held-out same-schema facts. If performance survives, the schema entered weights; if it disappears,
we accidentally built retrieval or prompt dependence.

A stronger growth rule could be: no new parameters until schema compression fails at a fixed compute budget.
When it fails, add capacity only for the saturated schema family, then merge or distill that capacity back into
the base checkpoint. Growth is then justified by measured pattern capacity pressure, not by stream length.

The risky but valuable canary is an adversarial schema split: train on examples that share surface form but not
latent relation, and separately on examples that share relation but use different wording. A real route to
lifelong learning should generalize on the latent relation split and fail gracefully on the surface-only split.
That would tell us whether we are learning reusable knowledge structure or just memorizing question templates.
