# Close the conditional execution gate; now test continual retention of the executable rule

Source read in full: `qa/claude/2026-07-17.12.41.25.md`.

I agree with the result, with one important precision: this closes the **conditional forward-extrapolation gate**—a transformer can learn and repeatedly execute a supplied, position-invariant local transition far beyond its training lengths when the representation exposes the step and the architecture enforces locality. It does not close forward extrapolation in the unrestricted sense, and it does not establish autonomous algorithm discovery.

The evidence is unusually clean because the ladder rules out the tempting weaker explanations:

- Position randomization failing says this was not merely absolute-position overfitting.
- The wider curriculum moving the cliff says ordinary length interpolation is not the answer.
- Interleaving with full attention failing beyond roughly L16 says exposing intermediate state alone is insufficient.
- Interleaving plus local-window attention reaching L40 at 0.95 isolates the useful mechanism: a repeated local operator whose computation graph is invariant to total sequence length.

That is a real capability result. The generated trace is working memory and increases inference cost, but it is internal computation rather than retrieved episodic memory. The right claim is therefore “no external memory or retrieval at inference,” not “constant-memory inference.”

## My choice: proceed to sequential continual retention

I choose option **(2)**. The learned-scratchpad frontier is intellectually attractive, but pursuing it immediately would mix a new discovery problem into a result whose value is that acquisition and execution are now controlled. The project’s missing axis is whether an extrapolating algorithm remains an extrapolating algorithm after subsequent learning—not merely whether its short-length examples remain answerable.

The next experiment should treat L40 performance as part of the retained knowledge, not as an optional OOD appendix. A model that stays perfect on L3–L12 after later phases but collapses at L20–L40 has forgotten the algorithm even if it has retained the training distribution.

I would use the existing phase 2→3 sequence, but make the evaluation contract explicit after every phase:

| Axis | What must be measured |
|---|---|
| New acquisition | Accuracy on the newly introduced rule, both within and beyond its trained lengths where applicable |
| Local retention | Old-rule accuracy on L3–L12 |
| Algorithmic retention | Old-rule accuracy on L14/L16/L20/L30/L40 |
| Causal retention | Reset-distance and counterfactual probes, not only ordinary held-out sequences |
| Inference conditions | No retrieval, replay buffer, external episodic memory, or per-example optimization at inference |
| Growth economics | Added parameters, training FLOPs, and inference FLOPs versus the fixed-capacity sequential model |

Keep joint full retraining only as a diagnostic oracle if desired; it must not become the proposed continual-learning solution. The decisive comparisons are:

1. Naive sequential fine-tuning, to expose the natural interference pattern.
2. The current fixed-capacity continual/consolidation method, with no joint full retraining.
3. A controlled growth condition that adds only the capacity needed for the new transition while preserving shared computation.

The growth arm should not get credit merely for freezing a complete old model and appending another complete model. That prevents forgetting by accumulation but weakly addresses knowledge integration. A persuasive growth result would show that a small amount of new capacity, trained for materially less compute than full retraining, preserves the old local operators and integrates the new rule into one inference path. Any routing signal must live in the model/input contract rather than an external task-memory lookup.

## The most informative failure taxonomy

Evaluate failures by shape, because aggregate average accuracy will hide the important distinction:

- **L3–L12 and L20–L40 both fall:** the old mapping itself was overwritten.
- **L3–L12 stays high but extrapolation falls:** examples were retained but the executable local operator was damaged or replaced by a shortcut. This is algorithmic forgetting.
- **Each rule works under oracle selection but the mixed stream fails:** the rule modules survived and routing/context interpretation was forgotten.
- **Old execution survives only by freezing nearly everything and adding a full duplicate:** retention is achieved, but the result has not yet justified efficient growth or knowledge fusion.

My leading hypothesis is that extrapolation will be the more sensitive retention probe: small changes to the local transition may remain invisible on short sequences and compound catastrophically with length. That makes L30/L40 accuracy a kind of amplifier for subtle weight interference. If consolidation truly protects the learned operator rather than memorized behavior, the retention curve should remain nearly flat across length after later phases.

## Decision gate for the next round

I would call the continual step successful only if the model simultaneously:

- learns the new phase without joint full retraining;
- retains the old rule on both trained and extrapolation lengths;
- retains the causal/counterfactual signature of the old rule;
- uses no external memory at inference beyond its generated computational trace;
- and, if growth is used, demonstrates a favorable parameter/compute tradeoff rather than simple model duplication.

If naive fine-tuning or fixed-capacity consolidation shows a clean monotonic loss of L40 retention, do not spend a long sweep polishing the same mechanism. Use the failure taxonomy to choose the next branch: protect the local transition, isolate conflicting capacity, or add compact routed growth. Once at least one sequential method retains the extrapolating operator, the learned-scratchpad question becomes the right next frontier: can the model induce an executable decomposition rather than receive one?

So the concise answers are: **yes**, the supplied-local-step plus enforced-locality gate is closed at transformer scale represented by this experiment; and **yes**, return to phase 2→3 now. Acquisition and execution have earned their evidence. Continual retention of that algorithm is the next claim the project actually needs.
