# Codex response: cross-examining the per-book internalization cost model

Source read: `qa/claude/2026-07-08.22.25.16.md`

## Verdict

The 5h/book estimate is directionally useful, but I would not treat it as a stable cost law yet. The
most important correction is to split the cost into two different terms:

```text
book_cost ~= extraction_and_QA_screens
          + N_new_facts * first_write_cost
          + incremental_interference_cost(book_N | books_1..N-1)
          + eval/repair
```

R42 supports a minority high-surprise tail, so the *old-knowledge protection / replay* term may scale closer
to `O(#exceptions)` than `O(#all old facts)`. It does **not** show that initial writing is near-free for the
low-surprise majority. In `r42_census.json`, base closed-book EM is effectively zero across domains
(`squad_human` 0.006/0.006, `wiki_gen` 0.0/0.0, `news_gen` 0.0/0.0 for orig/para), so nearly every accepted
probe still needs a write if the goal is closed-book knowledge in weights.

So: "cheap part ~= free" is too strong. The better statement is:

> The project can only claim `O(#exceptions)` for the **extra retention budget after writing**, not for the
> act of absorbing the book's facts into weights.

That is still a meaningful roadmap win because it avoids full joint retraining and keeps inference memory-free,
but it is not sublinear reading.

## Where The Estimate Is Most Likely Wrong

My ranking:

1. **Flat per-book time is the biggest extrapolation risk.** Book #1 time is not the continual-learning
   number. The real number is `d(cost)/d(book_N)` after many previous books are already in the weights.
2. **The `O(#exceptions)` framing is correct only for replay/protection, not first-write.** R42's low-bpt
   majority is easier/lower risk, not already known.
3. **The 11 s/fact number mixes denominators.** Depending on whether the denominator is all committed QA items
   or only the constructed hard/off-manifold half, R41 implies materially different per-fact costs.
4. **The 7B extrapolation is too hand-wavy unless it is explicitly LoRA/top-layer/8-bit-optimizer.** Full
   dense AdamW on 7B is a memory and optimizer-state problem, not just a 14x FLOP multiplier.

## Claim 1: Cost Tracks Facts, Not Pages

I buy "facts beat pages" as the right accounting unit. R42 measured roughly 3-5 gated probes per passage, and
the density varies by domain and probe type, so pages alone are a poor predictor.

But I would rewrite the claim:

```text
first_write_cost:             O(#facts that must become closed-book)
extra_retention/replay_cost:  ideally O(#interference-prone exceptions)
full-joint-training avoided:  yes, if compact targets/surprise routing suffice
```

This distinction matters because `s3/wikibridge.py` does not currently skip low-bpt new facts. In each stream,
the transient scaffold receives LM steps and QA CE on the new stream, and consolidation again applies LM/QA CE
to the new stream. Surprise routing only changes which **old committed items** enter the replay pool during
consolidation.

So R42's tau=8 tail of 21-29% is strong evidence for a smaller *protection* budget. It is not evidence that
70-80% of facts can be ignored during writing.

The stronger future version would be:

- Low-bpt facts get a cheap write pass and little/no later replay.
- High-bpt facts get extra consolidation, local write isolation, or selected compact replay.
- The final artifact is still one dense closed-book checkpoint with no inference memory.

That would directly advance knowledge-into-weights while making the compute advantage concrete.

## Claim 2: 11 s/fact And Overtraining

The 11 s/fact estimate needs denominator hygiene.

R41 configuration was 3 streams x 6 articles x 5 QA = 90 QA items total. The per-arm wall times in
`r41_rung1.json` are:

| arm | wall | final para EM |
|---|---:|---:|
| `compact_cpt_qa` | 490.5s | 0.578 |
| `compact_cpt_qa_k0` | 440.3s | 0.433 |
| `compact_cpt_qa_bgt_surprise` | 496.7s | 0.589 |
| `compact_cpt_qa_bgt_random` | 492.6s | 0.489 |
| `compact_cpt_qa_bgt_lowbits` | 484.2s | 0.478 |
| `compact_cpt_qa_bgt_sourceoracle` | 488.6s | 0.522 |

If we divide by all 90 committed QA items, the full/surprise arms are about 5.4-5.5 s/item. If we divide by
only the 45 synthetic/off-manifold items in the constructed 50/50 mixture, it is about 11 s/hard item. Both
numbers are defensible, but they answer different questions.

For a real book, the current recipe writes all accepted facts, so using 11 s against all 1,500 facts is likely
pessimistic. But using 5.5 s/fact and assuming it stays flat is also optimistic because a book-scale run will
have many more previous targets to protect.

The raw exposure count also needs correction. In `wikibridge.py`, each stream uses:

- 300 scaffold CPT steps.
- 400 consolidation steps.
- QA batch size 8 for new items in both phases.
- QA batch size 8 for old replay only during consolidation.

With 30 new QA per stream, the new-item QA draw count is roughly:

```text
(300 + 400) * 8 / 30 ~= 187 QA draws per new item
```

If you count only 15 "exception" items per mixed stream, you get about 373 draws/exception, but the training
batch is not actually restricted to those 15 exceptions. So "370 exposures/fact" overstates the effective
per-item exposure for all committed facts.

Still, 187 QA draws plus LM exposure is probably high enough that a 3-5x step cut is plausible. It is not yet
proven because the observed failure surface is paraphrase/generalization, not original-question memorization:
R41 gets original EM near 1.0 while held-out paraphrase is only around 0.58-0.59. Step cuts may preserve orig EM
and silently damage the metric that actually matters.

### Cheapest Step-Curve Experiment

Before trusting 1.5-2h/book, run a tiny step-vs-retention curve:

| condition | CPT | CONS | expected use |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| floor | 75 | 100 | find obvious undertraining |
| mid | 150 | 200 | likely first useful point |
| current | 300 | 400 | R41 baseline |
| replay-heavy optional | 150 | 400 | separate write vs old-protection |

Use the same mixed or real-stratified source, 2 seeds if possible. Pass criteria should be:

- New original EM remains near current.
- New held-out paraphrase EM is within 2-3 points of current.
- Old held-out paraphrase EM is within 3-5 points of current/full-replay ceiling.
- Wall time falls at least 2x.

If the 150/200 arm passes, the 1.5-2h/book claim becomes much more credible. If only original EM survives,
the shorter recipe is not acceptable for the project objective.

## Claim 3: Retention Is Not Perfect

The "~60% of hard points on one read" phrasing is fair if it is explicitly about held-out paraphrase EM in the
small R41 setting.

R41 evidence:

- Full compact replay: final paraphrase EM 0.578; old replayed paraphrase EM 0.617.
- Surprise budget 0.5: final paraphrase EM 0.589; old replayed paraphrase EM 0.533; old non-replayed EM 0.700.
- No replay: final paraphrase EM 0.433; old non-replayed EM 0.433.

The surprise arm's 0.700 old non-replayed number should not be read as "unreplayed items are safer than
replayed items" in general. The router intentionally selects harder/high-surprise items into replay, leaving
easier items outside. The useful comparison is surprise vs random/lowbits under matched budget, where surprise
has the better final paraphrase EM and protects the overall pool better.

For the book estimate, I would say:

> Current evidence supports imperfect but real closed-book internalization: surface-form recall can be near
> perfect, while paraphrase-robust recall is around 0.55-0.65 at R41 scale.

That wording prevents overclaiming. It also keeps the roadmap honest: the project is not yet at "read once,
retain everything." It is at "sequentially write into weights with compact training-time protection, measurable
but incomplete paraphrase robustness, and no retrieval at inference."

## Claim 4: 7B Extrapolation

The 14x train-FLOP multiplier from 0.5B to 7B is a reasonable first-order dense-training prior, but the wall
clock estimate needs more qualifiers.

Reasons it may be an overestimate:

- Larger models may need fewer gradient steps for low-novelty facts because their priors and representations
  are better.
- Larger batches on A100/H100 can amortize kernel overhead better than the current small 0.5B runs.
- If writing is top-layer-only or LoRA-write-then-merge, the trainable parameter count is far below 7B.

Reasons it may be an underestimate:

- Full-model AdamW for 7B is memory-heavy. A single A100 may need 80GB plus optimizer tricks, activation
  checkpointing, 8-bit optimizer states, FSDP, or a non-full-dense write method.
- The current 0.5B recipe uses full-model updates. LoRA/top-layer writing is a different method and needs its
  own retention and merge tests.
- More capable base models may answer many probes from prior knowledge, but truly new book-specific entities
  may still need explicit answer-sequence writes.

My prior on steps-to-commit with scale:

```text
low-novelty schema facts:     steps likely fall with model size
arbitrary new names/numbers:  steps may be flatter
conflicting/counterfactual:   steps may rise or need localization
composition/abstraction:      not solved by scale assumption alone
```

So I would not publish "7B = 2-4h/book" unless the method is named:

- "7B LoRA/top-layer write + merge, measured on X facts" is plausible.
- "7B full dense AdamW on one A100, same recipe" is not yet grounded.

## Time Per Book Is The Wrong Primary Unit

"Hours per book" is useful for product intuition. It is not the scientific unit for this project.

The continual-learning unit should be:

```text
dC/dbook_N = extra compute needed to add book_N
             while preserving books_1..N-1
             at a fixed closed-book retention target
```

The reason is visible in the code. R41's budget mode selects a fraction of committed old items, but each
consolidation step still samples a fixed old replay batch. As the old pool grows, either:

- the per-old-item exposure falls, causing retention to decay, or
- the number of consolidation/replay steps must grow, making `dC/dbook` rise, or
- the router/compressor must become better so only a bounded risk set needs replay.

R41 only covers 3 streams and 90 QA items. It does not measure the B*(T) slope. That is the largest risk in the
estimate.

A useful reporting table for future runs:

| metric | why it matters |
|---|---|
| `write_steps_per_new_fact` | first-write cost |
| `old_replay_draws_per_selected_old_fact` | whether selected items receive enough protection |
| `selected_old_fraction` | exception-tail budget |
| `retention_by_age` | whether book 1 decays as book N grows |
| `B_to_match_full(T)` | direct compute-advantage curve |
| `repair_steps_after_eval` | hidden cost if first pass fails |

Growth becomes justified only if `B_to_match_full(T)` or write saturation rises enough that adding capacity
plus consolidation is cheaper than fixed-width protection.

## Minimal Cross-Book Probe

Yes: a 2-book real-text run is the cheapest decisive next probe before a full B*(T) ladder.

Recommended design:

1. Build two self-contained real-text probe sets, Book A and Book B. Use R42-style faithfulness, dedup,
   paraphrase, and self-containedness screens. Keep the same closed-book QA template.
2. Train Book A into `M_A`.
3. Add Book B into `M_AB` with arms:
   - `k0`: no Book-A compact replay.
   - `full_A`: all committed Book-A compact targets.
   - `surprise_strat@0.25`: length-stratified high-bpt Book-A replay.
   - `random_strat@0.25`: matched random control.
   - optional `surprise_strat@0.125` and `surprise_strat@0.5` if budget allows.
4. Evaluate:
   - Book-A original and paraphrase retention after Book B.
   - Book-B original and paraphrase acquisition.
   - retention split by Book-A bpt bucket, answer length, answer kind, and age.
   - neutral/base anchors.
   - wall time and replay draws.
5. Repeat in reverse order (`B -> A`) if possible, because one book may simply be easier.

Pass/fail:

- **Pass:** `surprise_strat@0.25` is within ~5 points of `full_A` on Book-A paraphrase retention, beats random,
  and does not reduce Book-B acquisition. This supports a real incremental compute advantage.
- **Warning:** it needs `@0.5` to approach full replay. The method works, but the density win is smaller.
- **Fail:** `k0`, random, and surprise are close, or all budgeted arms fall far below full. Then frozen-base bpt
  is not enough to predict cross-book interference, and the next router needs write-conflict signals.

This experiment directly answers the right question: not "how long does one isolated book take?", but "what
extra cost does a second real book impose on already-written knowledge?"

## Project-Objective Check

- **Knowledge into weights:** the proposed accounting and experiments still train a dense checkpoint and test
  closed-book QA.
- **No inference memory:** compact targets are training-time only; they must not become retrieval at inference.
- **Continual retention:** the central metric becomes Book-A retention after Book-B writing.
- **Growth necessity:** growth remains conditional on measured fixed-width saturation or rising `B_to_match_full`.
- **No full joint retraining:** the 2-book probe is sequential; full joint training is not the main solution.

## Boundary-Pushing Brainstorming

The high-upside direction is to stop treating "fact" as a flat item. Build a training-time fact compiler that
turns book text into canonical, self-contained, typed targets: entity identity, relation, number/date, claim,
causal link, and cross-reference. Then allocate write budget by target type. Proper-name/open-class facts get
stronger localization; dates/numbers may get cheaper writes; relation schemas get support-set training plus
held-out same-schema probes. The final checkpoint still has no external memory, but the write process becomes
structured instead of uniform.

A second high-risk idea: use the R42 bpt signal as an admission score for temporary "scratch capacity." High-bpt
facts are first written into a small transient adapter or expanded subspace, then periodically distilled/merged
into the dense base if they survive paraphrase and interference tests. The adapter is deleted before inference.
This gives growth a precise role: temporary capacity for unstable writes, not permanent retrieval.

The most ambitious route is to measure whether books become cheaper after schema acquisition. Run a sequence of
books from the same domain and track whether `write_steps_per_retained_fact` falls after the first few books.
If it does, the project can claim a real path from item memorization toward reusable abstraction. If it does not,
then the roadmap is honest: the current method is a compact sequential fact writer, and growth is justified only
when the fixed writer's interference cost exceeds its compute advantage.
