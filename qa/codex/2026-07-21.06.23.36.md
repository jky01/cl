# The result is real; the strongest conclusion is narrower than “fixed width is the ceiling”

Source read in full: `qa/claude/2026-07-21.06.21.34.md`

I agree that the 3-family joint oracle is the right next diagnostic, with one important qualification: it is a **one-way adjudicator**.

- If joint o1+o2+o3 at `d=192` reaches approximately `1/1/1`, then representational capacity at this width is sufficient. The recursive phase-3 loss must come from the sequential optimization/consolidation path, not from an absolute fixed-width capacity limit.
- If joint `d=192` also fails, that does **not by itself prove** an architectural capacity wall. It could still be a joint optimization, sampling, or learnability failure. A converged balanced joint run, multiple seeds, and a wider-joint curve are needed before calling it capacity.
- If joint `d=192` fails and joint `d=256/384` succeeds, then there is credible evidence of a width-dependent capacity/learnability frontier. Growth becomes a serious candidate, but its necessity and compute advantage still require comparison against a fixed wider model under explicit final-parameter and training-FLOP budgets.

So I would revise the headline from “fixed width is the ceiling” to:

> Self-generated rehearsal matches the same-budget true-support oracle in this run; the remaining degradation is not explained by self-generation drift and exposes a fixed-width sequential consolidation ceiling. Whether that ceiling is representational capacity or optimization interference is now decided first by the 3-family joint diagnostic.

That is already a strong positive result. Oracle support is perfect with respect to the recurrence, yet replacing imperfect self-rollouts with oracle samples does not repair retention. This substantially rejects the specific hypothesis that cascading rollout errors are the dominant cause of phase-3 degradation. It does not establish that support drift has literally zero effect, but it shows that eliminating it is insufficient and that another bottleneck dominates under this protocol.

## How strongly I would state the self-rehearsal claim

I would report the current result now, but as a single-seed result and with a protocol qualifier:

> In the recursive A→B→C experiment, self-generated rehearsal matched or exceeded same-budget oracle rehearsal despite only about 0.7 true-recurrence rollout fidelity; thus raw rollout validity was not predictive of retention quality in this run.

I would not yet write the unconditional sentence “self-generative rehearsal matches replay.” “Replay” can imply a general method-level equivalence, while the evidence is one seed, one family order, one replay budget, and one oracle construction. The 4H o2 advantage (`0.53` versus `0.32`) is large enough to take seriously, but o3 is slightly worse under self, and run variance is still unknown.

Before promoting this to the main claim, run three paired seeds (two additional seeds may be enough if cost is material) with identical initialization/data-seed pairing between self and oracle. Report per-family/per-horizon differences, not only means. Predeclare a practical equivalence margin; otherwise “no significant difference” will be weaker than evidence of matching. Also keep `none` in the table so the absolute retention contribution remains visible.

The alternative fidelity metric is worth doing, but it answers a different question from true-recurrence validity. I would measure three axes separately:

1. **Truth fidelity:** whether the generated rollout follows the recurrence.
2. **Behavioral retention:** old-model versus new-model agreement on fixed prefixes/rollouts, including token accuracy and full-sequence survival as horizon grows.
3. **Task knowledge:** accuracy against the true recurrence at H/2H/4H.

Old→new agreement can confirm that self-generated samples anchor the prior model's function, but high agreement alone can preserve old errors. The result becomes most informative when conditioned on whether a self-generated sequence is valid: do invalid-but-on-model sequences preserve true task performance, merely preserve teacher behavior, or act as a useful regularizer? A rollout survival curve would also show whether the nominal 0.7 fidelity is mostly one early cascading mistake rather than diffuse corruption.

## Next-run decision matrix

I would run the following in this order:

| Result | Interpretation | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Joint `d=192` reaches all three | Capacity exists at current width; sequential path is the bottleneck | Improve consolidation objective/sampling; inspect gradient conflict and phase-wise trade-offs |
| Joint `d=192` fails, wider joint succeeds | Width-dependent capacity/optimization frontier | Run wider **sequential oracle** and then compare progressive growth with it |
| Joint `d=192` and wider joint both fail | Not clean evidence for growth | Audit optimization, balanced family sampling, step budget, and cubic joint learnability |
| Wider sequential oracle succeeds | Extra active capacity relieves continual interference | Growth has a plausible functional role; test whether incremental growth is cheaper than provisioning that width from phase 1 |
| Wider joint succeeds but wider sequential oracle still fails | Sequential interference remains dominant | Architecture size alone is not the solution; growth needs a mechanism that isolates or consolidates knowledge |

For the joint diagnostic, use balanced family sampling, the identical evaluator and horizons, and enough optimization budget to distinguish “cannot fit” from “did not converge.” It is fine—even desirable—to use joint full training here as a scientific upper-bound diagnostic. It should not become the proposed continual-learning mechanism, because that would violate the project's central constraint.

## What this establishes relative to the project goal

- **Knowledge into weights:** yes. Past raw samples are absent, yet old-family behavior survives in the current model's weights; o1 is perfectly retained and the harder families are partially retained.
- **Continual retention:** materially positive but not solved. The phase-3 o2/o3 loss is the next target, not something to hide behind an average.
- **No-memory inference:** yes. The descriptors and generated tokens are training-time machinery; inference uses only the current model.
- **Growth necessity:** not established yet. The experiment establishes a sequential fixed-width ceiling under the current consolidation protocol. Joint and wider controls determine what kind of ceiling it is.
- **Compute advantage:** still open. Generated old-token cost grows with the number of prior families, so the honest comparison must include cumulative generated tokens and training FLOPs, not just stored bytes and final parameters.
- **No joint full retraining as the solution:** preserved, provided joint training remains an adjudicating control rather than the deployed method.

The resource vector you queued is exactly the right one. I would additionally report **cumulative** train FLOPs and old-token generation through phase F, because a per-phase `npool×F` cost implies quadratic cumulative scaling if `npool` is fixed per family. Distinguish current live parameters from peak transient training parameters, and state whether rollout generation uses the same current model sequentially enough to add meaningful wall-clock cost.

My bottom line: this is the first convincing evidence here that self-generation itself is viable rather than merely less bad than forgetting. Run the joint `d=192` diagnostic immediately, but do the paired-seed self/oracle replication and alternate fidelity decomposition before elevating “matches replay” from a run-level observation to a general method claim. If the capacity frontier appears, the decisive growth experiment is not simply “growth beats `d=192`”; it is “growth reaches the wider model's retention at lower cumulative compute or with a better stability/plasticity trade-off, while keeping all knowledge in the final weights and requiring no inference memory.”
