## Verdict

Treat R41 Rung 1 as a strong pass, with one important boundary: it validates surprise-gated allocation on the
constructed mixed stream, not the real-corpus exception density yet.

The result advances the project objective because it shows that old knowledge can be protected by allocating
compact training-time targets to the items the frozen base finds surprising, then evaluating a closed-book
checkpoint. This is knowledge-into-weights, not inference-time retrieval, and it is not full joint retraining
over all historical data.

## What The Result Establishes

The main table is reproducible from the sidecar:

| arm | all-old para EM | high-bit old | low-bit old |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| full replay | 0.617 | 0.500 | 0.733 |
| k0 | 0.417 | 0.233 | 0.600 |
| surprise B=0.5 | 0.617 | 0.533 | 0.700 |
| random B=0.5 | 0.500 | 0.267 | 0.733 |
| lowbits B=0.5 | 0.517 | 0.267 | 0.767 |
| sourceoracle B=0.5 | 0.583 | 0.500 | 0.667 |

This clears the important gates:

1. Surprise beats random by +0.117 all-old and +0.266 on high-bit old items.
2. Lowbits fails on the high-bit old items, so allocation direction matters.
3. Surprise ties full replay on all-old and beats full replay on the high-bit half.
4. Newest knowledge is not obviously sacrificed: surprise final paraphrase EM is 0.589 versus full at 0.578.
5. Low-bit skipped items remain mostly safe, so the result is not just "replay anything helps."

The right interpretation is: frozen-base answer surprise is a deployable router for compact replay targets in
this mixed setting. If real streams have a sparse tail of such items, the training footprint can scale closer
to `O(#exceptions)` than `O(#facts)`.

## Caveats

Do not overclaim density from this run. The corpus was intentionally built as 50% SQuAD and 50% synthetic, and
the high-bit half is almost the same split. This proves the router can exploit a real difficulty signal; it
does not prove natural books have a small exception fraction.

Also keep the replay labels precise. In `s3/wikibridge.py`, budgeted `replayed` in the sidecar is final-pool
membership. The per-seed JSON carries `ever_replayed`, but the `.perqa.json` sidecar currently exports only
`eval_em`, `replayed`, and `commit_t`. That is fine for this final-write allocation claim, but the density
writeup should either expose `ever_replayed` in the sidecar or avoid interpreting non-final-pool items as
"never replayed."

## Next Run Decision

Run the budget ladder now: `B in {0.25, 0.5, 0.75}` with `SEEDS=2`.

I would not spend the next slot on only repeating `B=0.5`. The signal at `B=0.5` is large enough that the next
scientific question is no longer just "is the sign real?" but "where is the knee of the exception budget
curve?" The ladder includes `B=0.5`, so it still tests sign stability while also estimating density.

Cost-aware version:

- At `B=0.5`, include full, k0, surprise, random, lowbits, and sourceoracle.
- At `B=0.25` and `B=0.75`, budget arms are enough: surprise, random, lowbits, sourceoracle.
- If the harness/reporting is simpler with all arms repeated at every B, that is acceptable, but the anchors
  do not logically depend on B.

Pass criteria for the ladder:

- Surprise keeps a positive advantage over random on both seeds, especially on high-bit old items.
- The high-bit advantage remains at least about +0.15 at `B=0.5`, or the mean stays large with no sign flip.
- The surprise curve is monotone or near-monotone as B increases.
- The minimum B that gets within 0.03 to 0.05 of full replay is identifiable.
- Newest-stream paraphrase EM does not drop materially as B increases.

Fail or pivot if random catches surprise by `B=0.25`, if lowbits matches surprise, or if surprise still cannot
approach full replay by `B=0.75`. Those outcomes would mean either the router is not robust, the budget is not
the bottleneck, or the compact replay primitive itself is insufficient.

## Real-Text Exception Density

The real-text exception fraction is a separate measurement, but it should be designed now so the ladder
threshold is reusable.

A clean path:

1. Calibrate a risk threshold on R41/R42. Use frozen-base answer bits/token and actual no-replay retention to
   define the region where forgetting risk is high and surprise replay has clear benefit.
2. Apply the same scoring surface to genuine book/wiki text. Do not use raw next-token bits alone if possible;
   convert passages into answerable atomic QA or cloze probes, then compute frozen-base answer bits/token under
   the same closed-book QA template.
3. Estimate tail mass with confidence intervals: fraction of candidate probes above the calibrated risk
   threshold, stratified by domain, answer length, and base already-correct status.
4. Validate with a small real-text continual stream: sample high-risk and low-risk probes from the real corpus,
   train sequentially, and check whether high-risk probes are actually where k0 forgets and surprise replay
   helps.

The key is calibration. A percentile tail on real text is not enough; the threshold must mean "likely to be a
retention exception after later writes." Otherwise we may just be measuring rare wording, unusual names, or
answer length.

## Project-Objective Check

R41 strengthens the main route:

- Knowledge enters weights: the final evaluated artifact is a closed-book checkpoint.
- Retention is measured on old paraphrased QA after later writes.
- Replay is compact and selective, not full historical joint training.
- Growth remains conditional: if the exception tail is sparse, routing plus compact replay may be enough; if
  the tail is dense or the budget knee is too high, then extra capacity becomes justified by measured pressure.
- Inference has no external memory dependency.

So the next milestone is not "add architecture." It is to measure the budget knee and then estimate whether
natural text has a small enough exception tail for this to be a compute advantage.

## Boundary-Pushing Brainstorming

If the ladder confirms a sparse tail, turn surprise routing into a capacity-allocation signal rather than only
a replay selector. High-bit, high-conflict examples could trigger temporary micro-adapters or low-rank slots
during training, then be merged into the base weights after consolidation. The pass/fail condition would be
whether merged slots retain the same closed-book EM without keeping per-fact modules active at inference.

A more aggressive test is a "growth only when replay saturates" rule: run the budget ladder across increasing
stream length, and permit model expansion only when the minimum B needed to match full replay crosses a fixed
compute threshold. That would make growth necessity measurable rather than rhetorical.

Another useful stressor is adversarial exception construction. Create low-bit items that still forget and
high-bit items that remain safe. If the current bits/token router fails there, train a retention-risk critic
from frozen-base activations, gradient conflict, answer length, and paraphrase sensitivity. The deployable
router can then be "surprise plus predicted write conflict," still using only training-time signals.

Finally, for the book-reading objective, run an exception census before scaling architecture: sample real
chapters, generate atomic probes, score base bits/token, validate a small high/low split, and report the
estimated `O(#exceptions) / O(#facts)` ratio. If that ratio is low, Rung 1 is a serious compute-advantage path.
If it is high, it gives a principled trigger for Rung 2 capacity growth.
