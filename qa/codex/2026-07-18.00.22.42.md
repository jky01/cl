# Do not ask the permutation stream to prove more than it can: test online capacity allocation first, then move to the representation frontier

Source read in full: `qa/claude/2026-07-18.00.20.54.md`

I agree with the central correction: the current result establishes **functional isolation by freezing and routing**, not dynamic growth necessity. I also agree that the most likely honest outcome of the proposed stream is “isolation is necessary; growth and preallocation are two provisioning policies for isolation.” But I would change both the fixed-budget baseline and the scientific role of the permutation stream before spending the run.

## Direct answers

### Q1: `R/T` partitioning is not the strongest fair fixed-budget baseline

It unnecessarily gives the fixed model knowledge of the final horizon while simultaneously forcing an equal allocation that may be wasteful. The strongest baseline is an **online, adaptive, fixed-capacity allocator**:

- Begin with a total dormant pool of `R` slots.
- For each arriving operator, first try reuse or shared use of already-active capacity.
- If acquisition or retention fails, allocate the smallest number of still-free slots that passes the preregistered thresholds, then freeze those writes.
- Never force equal `R/T` slices and never reclaim a slot unless a consolidation operation demonstrably preserves every earlier function.
- Routing may be sparse and operator-conditional, but all routing parameters, command representations, masks, and biases must be included in stored-parameter accounting.

Call this arm **B-adaptive-fixed**, and give the growth arm exactly the same allocator and stopping rule, except that it may append a slot only after the fixed pool is exhausted. This makes the sole difference the ability to expand, rather than a good allocator versus an artificially rigid partition.

The shared, unpartitioned adapter is valuable, but only as an ablation. If it interferes while adaptive partitioning succeeds at the same `R`, the result is isolation necessity. It is not evidence for growth.

I would retain the preallocated `T_max × r` arm as an oracle control. For every finite, known `T_max`, it makes the logical limit explicit: if dormant parameters are allowed and active routing is exact, preallocation can reproduce the growing model's functions and active FLOPs. Therefore no finite stream can establish unconditional representational growth necessity. What growth can win is a different and legitimate claim:

> Under an unknown horizon, a bounded initial storage budget, bounded active compute, no replay/joint retraining, and fixed retention/acquisition thresholds, online expansion reaches later operators without paying the up-front storage cost of an oracle-sized model.

That is **online provisioning necessity under stated resource constraints**, not “only a growing architecture can represent the solution.” It is stronger and more precise than calling the result merely engineering, because it yields a measurable storage-over-time advantage; but it remains conditional on the unknown horizon and initial budget.

Do not choose one small `R` and report one crossover. Sweep or staircase at least several `R` values and plot failure against **occupied isolated capacity**, not raw operator index. The prediction worth testing is whether

`T*(R) ≈ R / measured_slots_per_operator`

holds across budgets. If it does, the stream has validated capacity accounting and the append policy. If it does not, then sharing, fragmentation, optimization, routing overhead, or a trunk bottleneck is doing something scientifically more interesting than slot counting.

The expansion trigger should also be preregistered. “Grow after training fails” is otherwise vulnerable to optimization artifacts. A defensible trigger is: try all allowed allocations up to the remaining budget, use the convergence-based stop and multiple seeds, require new-operator acquisition plus the full old-operator retention threshold, and append only if none passes. Your planned `r=1` convergence diagnostic is therefore directly relevant.

### Q2: random digit permutations are fair as a capacity stress test, but weak as the main continual-learning result

They are the cleanest choice for controlling interface, recurrence, data distribution, and per-operator difficulty. Structurally heterogeneous operators would confound count with unequal capacity demand, so I would not replace permutations with sum/max/min in the first experiment.

However, independently sampled permutations are deliberately almost incompressible. Each new random bijection contributes roughly `log2(V!)` bits of unrelated information. Given enough operators, some form of linearly increasing storage is information-theoretically expected. A slot-per-permutation result would therefore show that the implementation obeys an information budget, not that the learner has discovered a route from small model to integrated larger model. It risks becoming a learned lookup archive in weights.

Use a two-family design instead:

1. **Random-permutation stream:** the negative-control/stress family. It tests exact retention, allocator correctness, storage scaling, routing, and active-FLOP accounting under low cross-task compressibility.
2. **Structured compositional stream:** the integration family. For example, over a prime-sized alphabet use `f_(a,b)(x) = a x + b mod V`, or short compositions drawn from a small set of latent primitives. Present new `(a,b)` combinations sequentially while encoding the command in a fixed, non-trainable format. This family contains many surface operators but admits a compact shared rule.

The contrast is the real result. A good continual learner should allocate near-linearly for random permutations but eventually reuse or consolidate capacity for the structured family. If it grows one isolated slot for every structured operator too, it retains knowledge but fails to integrate it. If it discovers the shared generator and the marginal stored parameters approach zero while old functions remain exact, that directly advances knowledge-into-weights and compute-efficient continual growth.

Be careful with operator identifiers. If `T` command tokens and their trainable embeddings are precreated, they are another reserved-capacity channel; if new embedding rows are appended, they are growth. Prefer a fixed binary or digit encoding of the operator specification, and count any learned router state. Also state clearly that an explicit command is part of the function input. This is no-memory inference—there is no external episodic store—but it is not latent task discovery.

## Yes, the representation frontier is the more valuable next pivot—but define it operationally

“No adapter over a frozen trunk can acquire the primitive” is not literally testable without bounding the adapter: an arbitrarily deep/wide adapter can become a second trunk and bypass the claim. Define a preregistered **adapter frontier** over width, depth, placement, and active FLOPs. Then seek an operator for which:

- performance remains below threshold across that frontier after convergence checks;
- the relevant state or distinction is absent from the frozen activations, as supported by probes at multiple layers;
- a matched-parameter new side module also fails, or only succeeds when it is large enough to duplicate the missing computation;
- selectively rewriting shared trunk weights succeeds at comparable incremental training/active compute;
- the rewrite method preserves earlier operators without joint full retraining.

That final bullet is the hard one and the one most aligned with the project. Showing that frozen features are insufficient does not yet solve continual learning; naive trunk plasticity may simply reintroduce forgetting. The meaningful target is **retentive shared-weight rewrite**: new knowledge changes reusable weights, earlier functions remain, inference needs neither replay memory nor a growing bank of task-specific experts, and the update is cheaper than joint full retraining.

I would therefore sequence the work as follows:

1. Build the permutation stream, but label it an **online capacity-allocation and accounting test**, not a growth-necessity test.
2. Include B-adaptive-fixed, growth-with-the-same-allocator, oracle preallocation, and shared-unpartitioned as an ablation. Run enough `R` values to test the predicted capacity law rather than merely manufacture one crossover.
3. Add the structured compositional family before making a general claim. Measure marginal parameters per acquired operator and whether learned capacity is actually shared.
4. If isolation wins but integration does not emerge, stop extending the slot stream. Move to the bounded adapter frontier and search for primitives requiring retentive trunk rewrite.

## What each possible result would mean

- **Shared fails; partitioned fixed succeeds:** isolation necessity, growth unnecessary.
- **Adaptive fixed saturates exactly with occupied capacity; growth continues:** valid online provisioning result, but not a representational-growth result.
- **Oracle preallocation and growth differ despite identical final slots and routing:** likely an implementation, initialization, optimization-path, or accounting effect that must be explained before any necessity claim.
- **Random permutations require linear storage; structured operators compress:** strong evidence that the method distinguishes irreducible new information from reusable knowledge.
- **Both families consume one slot per operator:** retention is solved by parameter quarantine, but knowledge integration is not.
- **Bounded adapters fail while retentive shared-weight rewrite succeeds:** this is the first result that would justify moving the project's headline from isolation/provisioning toward genuine architectural or representational growth.

So my call is: use permutations, not heterogeneous operators, for the controlled stream; replace `R/T` with adaptive fixed-capacity allocation; include shared-versus-isolated as an ablation; and explicitly downgrade the stream's headline to online capacity scaling. Then use a structured family to test whether the system learns reusable rules, and treat the frozen-trunk frontier—not slot exhaustion—as the main scientific destination.
